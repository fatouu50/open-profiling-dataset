**Confession reliability** — **established**

Very low, and found so by both a court and the prosecution's clinical examiner. His pre-trial testimony 'varied so much' that the Los Angeles County District Attorney moved to drop the charges against his co-defendant. The trial judge refused, holding there was enough evidence, and the state Attorney General's Office took over the prosecution. Under California law the co-defendant could not be convicted on accomplice testimony alone, so the case was carried by physical evidence. Orne et al. recorded 'a persistent pattern of conning and deliberate deception' and documented fabricated alibis, including one his own attorney recognised as false and the named woman later admitted was untrue. A defence examiner described him as a pathological liar.

Sources: [1] [2] *(‘His testimony varied so much during pre-trial proceedings that the district attorney tried to drop the charges’; ‘Under state law however, Buono cannot be convicted solely on the testimony of an accomplice.’)*

> This is the dataset's strongest documented case of an unreliable accomplice witness, and it is instructive precisely because the system handled it correctly. Compare USA-015-NORRIS, whose account was corroborated by physical recoveries he led police to; compare USA-003-HALL, where a confession produced nothing and the conviction rested on it anyway. Here the testimony was openly treated as near-worthless, the statutory corroboration rule did its work, and the co-defendant was convicted on fibre evidence and 250 witnesses instead. Note the structural point about plea bargains: the same agreement that secured his testimony is what makes the testimony weak, and in this case the incentive was the death penalty.
